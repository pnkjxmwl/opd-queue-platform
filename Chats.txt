I would frame the application like this:

A digital outpatient (OPD) queue management platform that lets patients remotely join hospital/doctor queues, track their live position and estimated consultation time, while giving hospitals and doctors a real-time system to operate those queues.

11. I would NOT let clients directly manipulate queue positions

This is an important architectural rule.

For example, the doctor UI shouldn't send:

move token 39 to position 12

and the frontend shouldn't decide what happens.

Instead, we create domain commands such as:

CALL_NEXT_PATIENT
START_CONSULTATION
COMPLETE_CONSULTATION
SKIP_PATIENT
MARK_NO_SHOW
PAUSE_SESSION
RESUME_SESSION
ADD_PRIORITY_PATIENT

The server validates the command.

For example:

Doctor requests:
CALL_NEXT_PATIENT

Backend checks:
- Is doctor logged in?
- Does doctor belong to this hospital?
- Is session active?
- Is there already a patient in consultation?
- Is there a next eligible patient?
- Is doctor allowed to call patient?

Only then does the server change the queue.

That prevents a huge class of bugs.

ABDM's current ecosystem supports ABHA, healthcare professional/facility registries, and consent-based exchange; its official materials also describe HFR/HPR as registries for facilities and professionals.

rather than making our queue system dependent on ABDM from day one.

That's an important strategic distinction.

28. Privacy architecture should start now, even though medical records aren't MVP

So even MVP data such as:

Gender
Hospital visits
Doctor

should be treated as sensitive personal information from an engineering perspective.

I'd therefore build:

into the architecture rather than bolting them on after launch.

And when we eventually introduce health records, we'll already have the foundation.

32. And there are now only a few really important product decisions left

I don't think we should start coding yet.

The next thing I would design is the exact queue engine.

Specifically, we need to settle things like:

A. How exactly is a token's position calculated?

B. Does "people ahead of you" include people who haven't checked in?

C. At what point does an unchecked remote patient become
   "must arrive now"?

D. Can a patient join if the doctor hasn't started the session?

E. Can a patient join after the scheduled OPD end time?

F. How many online tokens can a hospital issue?

G. Can staff insert walk-ins anywhere or only at the end?

H. How does emergency insertion affect everyone else's ETA?

I. What exactly happens when A21 is called but hasn't arrived?

J. What happens if A21 is still at home while A22 is checked in?

K. What if 10 patients reserve tokens but only 3 actually arrive?

L. What if the doctor takes 30 minutes on one patient?

M. What happens to tokens when the doctor changes?

N. What happens if the internet at the reception desk goes down?

O. What happens if two staff members simultaneously modify the queue?

P. What happens if the patient closes the app for two hours?

Q. What happens if the payment succeeds but the app crashes
   before showing the token?

Those questions are not edge-case trivia. Those are the actual product rules that will determine whether this queue system works in a real hospital.

And I think we should tackle them one by one next, starting with A–E: exactly how the virtual queue, check-in queue, OPD session timing, and ETA interact. That's the foundation on which the rest of the architecture should be built.
